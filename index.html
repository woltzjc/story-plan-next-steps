Part 1

Everything starts with your story.

What is the story you want your life to tell? This should guide every money decision you make from here.

Before you start: what you'll need
  • A recent pay stub
  • Your last 2–3 months of bank and credit card statements
  • Balances for your savings, retirement, and investment accounts
  • Statements for any debts (balance, interest rate, minimum payment)

Share finances with a spouse? Fill this out together. Rough numbers are fine to start. Everything saves on this device, so you can come back and fine-tune.

1

Name three themes

The three things you want to define the story of your life. Tap an idea or write your own.

Need help? Walk through a Life Vision exercise (10–15 minutes)
  1. Set the scene
    • Find a quiet spot and give yourself 10–15 minutes.
    • Married? Each do this on your own first, then compare. That conversation alone is worth it.
  2. Dream first, price tags later
    • Ignore money for now. Write fast and don't edit yourself.
    • At 80, looking back, what made it a good life?
    • What would you regret not doing?
    • When do you feel most like yourself?
    • What do you want more of in your life? Less of?
    • If you had a free year, how would you spend it?
    • What do you want the people you love to remember about you?
  3. Look for patterns
    • Read back what you wrote and circle the words and ideas that keep showing up.
    • Group the ones that belong together.
  4. Name your 3 themes
    • Give each group a one- or two-word name, like Memories, Generosity, or Freedom.
    • Check each one: Would I make a money decision based on this? Will it still matter in 10 years? If not, keep looking.
  5. Picture each theme in real life
    • For each theme, write one specific example of what it looks like, like “a birthday trip to Italy” or “giving monthly to a cause I love.”
    • These examples become the goals your plan pays for.
  6. Name what's in the way
    • For each theme, ask: What's standing in the way?
    • Be honest. Barriers aren't only money. They can be time, debt, not being on the same page with your spouse, or simply not giving yourself permission.
  7. Write your “from → to” line
    • Where does your story feel stuck today, and where do you want it headed?
    • Example: from “not gonna happen” to “I'm gonna make it happen!”

Don't overthink it. This isn't set in stone. Come back and revise it as life changes.

Part 2

Your story decides your plan.

Eight simple steps. Rough numbers are fine. You can always come back and fine-tune them.

Along the way you'll see a few common rules of thumb. They're general guidelines to help you get your bearings, not personal advice.

1

Gross income

Yearly income before taxes: your salary, or “gross pay” on your pay stub. Add a line for each person or source, like a spouse, side business, or rental property.

Name or sourcePer year

2

Savings, retirement, and investments

Add the accounts you have. Don't have one? Leave it off. That's what the plan is for.

Add an account
3

Debts and interest rates

Credit cards, car loans, student loans, and so on. The rate is the APR on your statement. You can leave your mortgage off if you'd rather; it's already in your housing expense.

4

Take-home pay

After taxes and paycheck deductions. Not sure? Look at a recent paycheck deposit in your bank account. Include any other money that lands in your account each month, like rental or side-business income after its expenses.
5

Monthly expenses

Look at the last few months of bank and card statements. Most people guess low.

6

A little extra margin for the unexpected

Life happens: car repairs, doctor visits, birthdays you forgot about. Set aside a cushion each month so a surprise doesn't derail the plan. Many people use 5–10% of their monthly expenses.

8

Use your story to decide how to use it

Not spend. Use. Give every dollar a job that points back to your three themes.

Not sure how to split it? Put most of it toward your top priority above, and give each story theme a small slice, even $25 a month. You can adjust anytime.

Helpful tools: a budgeting app (Jeremy uses YNAB, but use whatever works for you) and the Retirement tab in this tool.

Part 3

Your plan tells you your next step.

Overwhelm is real. Don't try to change everything at once. Pick just one to three clear next steps.

Ideas to get you started

Tap one to add it, then make it yours. Your steps will look different from anyone else's, based on your story and your plan.

Make it clear: say what, how much, and when. For example, “Move $200 to savings every payday, starting Dec 1.”

Done so far

    Make each one simple, attainable, and clear. When one is done, replace it with a new next step based on your story and your plan.
